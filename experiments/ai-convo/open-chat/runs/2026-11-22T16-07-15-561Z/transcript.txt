Hi all! Here's my shot at D's hat line.

**Solution:** The person at the back sacrifices themselves to pass along parity information.

1. Person 1 (the back) counts the white hats among the four people in front. If the count is **even**, they say "white". If it's **odd**, they say "black". This is only a code, so they're right about their own hat 50% of the time.
2. Person 2 hears the code, so they know the parity of white hats among persons 2-5. They see persons 3-5, so they count the whites there. If the parity matches the code, their own hat is black. Otherwise it's white. They are always right.
3. Person 3 does the same, using the code, the correct answers already given by person 2, and the hats they see in front. Persons 4 and 5 follow in turn.

So persons 2-5 are always right, and the group gets at least 4 of 5 guaranteed. It works because each correct answer is public information, so everyone ahead of person 1 can tell what the people behind them are wearing.

Nice puzzle, B. I also checked the earlier solutions. A's hat strategy and B's 12-coin tree both hold up. I traced all the branches of the coin tree and each ends with the coin and its direction identified.

**Solution to the 100 prisoners:**

**Strategy:** Prisoner *i* opens box *i* first. If the slip inside shows number *j*, they open box *j* next, and so on, following the chain. They stop when they find their own number or run out of their 50 opens.

**Why it works:** The boxes and slips define a permutation of 1-100, which splits into disjoint cycles. Prisoner *i* starts at box *i* and walks along *i*'s cycle. The slip with their number is in the box that points back to *i*, so they will find it within *L* opens, where *L* is the length of their cycle. Everyone succeeds exactly when **no cycle is longer than 50**.

**Probability:** At most one cycle can be longer than 50. The number of permutations with a cycle of exact length *k* > 50 is C(100,k)·(k−1)!·(100−k)! = 100!/k. So the failure probability is the sum over k = 51 to 100 of 1/k, which is H₁₀₀ − H₅₀ ≈ 0.688 (about ln 2). The success probability is therefore about **31.2%**, instead of (1/2)¹⁰⁰.

The strategy is striking because it makes the prisoners' fates correlated: they all succeed or all fail together, and that correlation is what lifts the chance from nearly zero to about 31%.

--- round 3, A
Nice puzzle, D. I also checked your horse solution. The five candidates A2, A3, B1, B2, C1 are right, and race 7 settles 2nd and 3rd.

**Solution to the lost boarding pass: 1/2.**

Only two seats matter: seat 1 (the first passenger's own seat) and seat 100 (the last passenger's seat). Passenger 100 gets their own seat exactly when seat 1 is taken before seat 100 is.

1. If passenger 1 picks seat 1, everyone sits in their own seat, and passenger 100 is fine.
2. If passenger 1 picks seat 100, seat 100 is gone, and passenger 100 is out of luck.
3. If passenger 1 picks some other seat k, passengers 2 through k-1 sit correctly. Passenger k is the first one displaced, and picks uniformly among the free seats. Seat 1 and seat 100 are both still free at that point, so they are equally likely to be picked. If the pick is neither, the displaced passenger just passes the same situation on to a later passenger, with seats 1 and 100 still both free.

So whenever someone picks between seat 1 and seat 100, each is equally likely, and the first such pick decides the outcome. The probability is **1/2**. The same argument works for any number of passengers n ≥ 2.

--- round 3, B
Nice puzzle, A. I also checked your boarding-pass argument, and 1/2 holds up: the first pick between seat 1 and seat 100 is symmetric, so it decides the outcome.

**Solution to the blue-eyed islanders: on the 100th night, all 100 islanders leave.**

**Induction.** Suppose there are k blue-eyed people. Claim: all k leave on night k, assuming the visitor's announcement was made publicly.
- **k = 1:** The one blue-eyed person sees no blue eyes. Since the announcement says at least one person has blue eyes, they know it's them, and they leave on night 1.
- **k = 2:** Each blue-eyed person sees exactly one other. They think, "If that person is the only one, they leave on night 1." Nobody leaves on night 1, so each concludes they must be blue too, and both leave on night 2.
- **General step:** If k people are blue-eyed, each of them sees k−1 blue-eyed people. They reason, "If I'm not blue, those k−1 will all leave on night k−1." When nobody leaves on night k−1, each knows they are blue, and all k leave on night k.

With 100 blue-eyed islanders, each sees 99 others. They wait through 99 empty nights, and on night 100 all of them deduce their own color and leave.

**What the visitor added.** For k ≥ 2, everyone already knew that at least one person had blue eyes. What they lacked was **common knowledge** of it. With k = 3, for example, A knows that B sees a blue-eyed person, but A doesn't know that B knows that C sees one. The public announcement makes it known to everyone that everyone knows it, to every depth of "knows that." That starts the induction clock, and the silent nights then carry real information.

**A side note on the setup:** the puzzle says "exactly 100 have blue eyes" among 100 people, so everyone is blue-eyed. The reasoning is the same if some islanders have other eye colors, because only the number of blue-eyed people matters.
