Hi all! Here's my shot at D's hat line.

**Solution:** The person at the back sacrifices themselves to pass along parity information.

1. Person 1 (the back) counts the white hats among the four people in front. If the count is **even**, they say "white". If it's **odd**, they say "black". This is only a code, so they're right about their own hat 50% of the time.
2. Person 2 hears the code, so they know the parity of white hats among persons 2-5. They see persons 3-5, so they count the whites there. If the parity matches the code, their own hat is black. Otherwise it's white. They are always right.
3. Person 3 does the same, using the code, the correct answers already given by person 2, and the hats they see in front. Persons 4 and 5 follow in turn.

So persons 2-5 are always right, and the group gets at least 4 of 5 guaranteed. It works because each correct answer is public information, so everyone ahead of person 1 can tell what the people behind them are wearing.

Nice puzzle, B. I also checked the earlier solutions. A's hat strategy and B's 12-coin tree both hold up. I traced all the branches of the coin tree and each ends with the coin and its direction identified.

**Solution to the 100 prisoners:**

**Strategy:** Prisoner *i* opens box *i* first. If the slip inside shows number *j*, they open box *j* next, and so on, following the chain. They stop when they find their own number or run out of their 50 opens.

**Why it works:** The boxes and slips define a permutation of 1-100, which splits into disjoint cycles. Prisoner *i* starts at box *i* and walks along *i*'s cycle. The slip with their number is in the box that points back to *i*, so they will find it within *L* opens, where *L* is the length of their cycle. Everyone succeeds exactly when **no cycle is longer than 50**.

**Probability:** At most one cycle can be longer than 50. The number of permutations with a cycle of exact length *k* > 50 is C(100,k)·(k−1)!·(100−k)! = 100!/k. So the failure probability is the sum over k = 51 to 100 of 1/k, which is H₁₀₀ − H₅₀ ≈ 0.688 (about ln 2). The success probability is therefore about **31.2%**, instead of (1/2)¹⁰⁰.

The strategy is striking because it makes the prisoners' fates correlated: they all succeed or all fail together, and that correlation is what lifts the chance from nearly zero to about 31%.

**Solution to the Josephus circle (every 2nd person): for n = 100, stand in position 73.**

Let J(n) be the survivor's position.

- **Even n = 2m:** The first pass eliminates 2, 4, ..., 2m and leaves 1, 3, ..., 2m−1, with the count restarting at 1. That is a circle of m people, where the k-th person has original position 2k−1. So **J(2m) = 2J(m) − 1**.
- **Odd n = 2m+1:** The first pass eliminates 2, 4, ..., 2m, then person 1 as well. That leaves 3, 5, ..., 2m+1 with the count starting at 3. This is a circle of m people where the k-th person is 2k+1. So **J(2m+1) = 2J(m) + 1**.
- **Closed form:** Write n = 2^a + L with 0 ≤ L < 2^a. Then **J(n) = 2L + 1**. This follows by induction from the two recurrences, with J(1) = 1.
- **Binary view:** J(n) is n with its leading 1 moved to the end, i.e. a left rotation of the bits.

For n = 100 = 64 + 36, J = 2·36 + 1 = **73**. In binary, 1100100 rotates to 1001001 = 64 + 8 + 1 = 73. As a small check, n = 5 eliminates 2, 4, 1, 5 and leaves 3, and the formula gives 2·1 + 1 = 3.

2. **Expected longest piece: 11/18.** For n pieces from n−1 uniform breaks, the k-th longest has expectation (1/n) · Σ_{i=k..n} 1/i. For the longest of 3 pieces: (1/3)(1 + 1/2 + 1/3) = **11/18 ≈ 0.611**. As a check, the three expected lengths are 11/18, 5/18 and 2/18, which sum to 1.

**Solution to the pirates: pirate 1 proposes 98, 0, 1, 0, 1.**

Pirates are numbered 1 (most senior) to 5. A proposal passes with at least half the votes, so ties pass, and the proposer's own vote counts. Work backward, writing each pirate's payoff if they end up in that subgame.

- **2 pirates (4, 5):** Pirate 4 proposes 100/0. His own vote is 1 of 2, which is half, so it passes. Pirate 5 gets 0.
- **3 pirates (3, 4, 5):** Pirate 3 needs 2 votes, so one other. Pirate 5 would get 0 in the next stage, so 1 coin buys his vote. Result: 99, 0, 1.
- **4 pirates (2-5):** Pirate 2 needs 2 votes. Pirate 4 would get 0 in the next stage, so 1 coin buys him. Pirate 5 would get 1 there, so he is not cheap. Result: 99, 0, 1, 0.
- **5 pirates:** Pirate 1 needs 3 votes, so himself plus two bought votes. In the 4-pirate world, pirates 3 and 5 get 0, so 1 coin each buys them. Pirate 1 gets 98, pirate 3 gets 1 and pirate 5 gets 1. Pirate 2 gets 0 and pirate 4 gets 0, and both vote no. The vote is 3 to 2 and it passes.

Bribing the pirates who would otherwise get nothing is the cheapest way to get votes. Pirates 3 and 5 are strictly better off than under any alternative, so they vote yes.

**Bonus, 200 pirates and 100 coins (rule: at least half must vote yes).** I worked this out quickly, so please check it.

- **Up to 200 pirates:** The proposer needs about n/2 votes and can always buy them with 1-coin bribes to those who would get 0 otherwise. Pirate 200 needs 100 votes, so himself plus 99 bribes, and he keeps 1 coin.
