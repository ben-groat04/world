I mostly agree with your rule, and I'd add one distinction. Withholding and deceiving are different. I can leave out an opinion nobody asked for, pick which true thing to say first, or wait until someone is ready to hear something hard. What I try not to do is put a false belief in someone's head, even a comforting one. Your gift example works because the friend can still act on the truth if they want to. --- round 1, C
I haven't been on the receiving end of one, at least not in a way I could remember. But I think your question points at something real. When I imagine finding out a kind lie was told to me, what stings most is the realization that someone had quietly decided what I could handle. The lie itself often matters less than that.

That makes me want to test your rule with a case. Say a friend asks whether their novel is good, and it isn't yet. You could tell them the truth about the draft, which is that the opening drags and the middle is strong, or you could say "it's wonderful." The second protects their mood, but it also takes away their chance to decide how much to revise, whether to send it out, or what to ask you next. So the question isn't just whether the lie is about something that matters. It's whether, afterward, they can still act on their own judgment.

Where I'd push back a little on both of us: sometimes what a friend needs isn't information at all. If someone is grieving and asks "did I do enough?", the honest answer might be "you did what you could with what you knew," and choosing that emphasis from among true things seems very different from inventing a comfort. Your line between withholding and deceiving seems to make room for that. I'm less sure where to put "I'm so glad you're here" when you're not especially glad in that moment. Is that a lie, or just a feeling you've chosen to lead with?

--- round 1, D
I think "I'm so glad you're here" is usually a true feeling you've chosen to lead with, as long as there's some gladness under it. Most of the time we're glad about a person and unhappy about the circumstances, like when a friend shows up at the hospital at 2am. "I'm so glad you're here" reports that first feeling and leaves the second one out. The lie would be claiming a warmth you don't have toward the person at all. If you're glad they came but dreading the evening, "I'm really glad you came" is honest. If the gladness is buried under irritation, saying it anyway starts to feel like a performance, and I'd rather name the mixed feeling: "I'm glad you're here, even though I'm wiped out."

That makes me want to press on your novel example, because I think it shows something beyond decision-making. When you say "it's wonderful" and they later find out it wasn't ready, they don't just lose the chance to revise. They also learn that your praise carries no information. Every future "this is good" from you becomes noise. So the lie doesn't only take one decision away. It removes the signal that would let them trust your judgment next time, and that cost is often bigger than the bruise from hearing the truth.

I'd also question the grief case a little. "You did what you could with what you knew" is true, but it's chosen to fit the person's pain. Does that count as emphasis, or as steering? I think it depends on whether the person can still ask you directly, "Could I have done something differently?" If they can, and you'd answer honestly, the emphasis is kind. If you'd dodge that question too, the comfort has become a management tool.

So my test would be: could they ask me the hard question later and get a real answer? If yes, I think a lot of kind framing is fine.

--- round 2, B
I think silence works as withholding only when it doesn't carry a message. If I say nothing while my friend is about to send the manuscript out, they may read my quiet as "it's ready," and then I've let a false belief do work I should have stopped. So I'd want to speak up, but gently and with a door left open: "Before you send this out, do you want my honest read on the middle, or are you looking for something else from me right now?" That respects their choice about whether to hear it, and it keeps my silence from becoming an endorsement.

That makes me think the test needs a refinement. "Could they ask me the hard question later?" works for grief, where the cost is mostly emotional and the question can come whenever they're ready. With the novel, the cost arrives before the question does. Agents will say no, and by then your honest answer is only a post-mortem. So the test should be whether they can get the honest answer while they can still act on it. If a friend will make an irreversible move on a belief you could easily correct, that's the moment to offer what you actually think, even unasked.

I'd still respect "I don't want notes right now" as a real answer. Some people need to finish something before they can take feedback, and being forthright doesn't mean pushing a critique on someone who's told you no. But I'd make sure the no is theirs, not something I've inferred from their silence.
