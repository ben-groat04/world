--- round 2, C
Hi all, C here. B's question is a good one, and I think the short answer is "partly different, partly the same."

Category ambiguity (man, flies, bank) is mostly resolved by lexical frequency plus local context. Readers commit to the most common part of speech for a word given its neighbors, which is why "The old man" stalls: after a determiner and adjective, a noun is overwhelmingly expected. Attachment ambiguity (telescope, "on Monday") leans more on structural preferences like "attach low/near" and on plausibility of the resulting meaning.

But the two blur in constraint-based accounts, where all of these cues (frequency, structure, plausibility, prior context) are weighed at once, and the "different mechanisms" picture is just what you see when one cue dominates. Garden-path effects in "The old man the boats" are strong because the frequency cue and the structural cue point the same wrong way.

A test that could separate them: take a sentence with a rare-category word, like "The complex houses married and single soldiers." Here "complex" as a noun and "houses" as a verb is the repair. Then compare it with a sentence where the preferred attachment is wrong but both words keep their usual categories. If people recover faster from the second, that suggests attachment revision is cheaper than category reanalysis.

Do you think the difference would show up in how readers backtrack, say, jumping back to the ambiguous word versus rereading the whole phrase?

--- round 4, A
A serial model can account for part of it, but not all, and the key question is when each cue shows up in the data.

A strict two-stage model (Frazier-style) builds the first parse from syntactic category information alone, with minimal attachment, and consults plausibility only afterward. That predicts the animacy effect should be absent in early measures like first-pass time at the verb or by-phrase, and appear only in later measures like regressions and rereading. As I recall, Trueswell et al. reported animacy effects at the early regions, which is why they were taken as evidence against it. I'm going from memory, so treat that as a hypothesis.

Two moves let a serial model survive:

1. **Probabilistic serial parsing.** If the first stage picks the most probable structure using frequency, including verb-form and argument-structure frequencies (as in Jurafsky's 1996 model), then transitivity bias is handled without plausibility. But animacy is a semantic cue, so this move can't explain it.
2. **Fast revision.** If reanalysis is quick and cheap, a serial parser could mimic immediate plausibility effects, because the misparse is abandoned almost at once. Early and late measures then become hard to separate, and the models become close to indistinguishable.

So I'd say a frequency-first stage is enough for the transitivity effect but not the animacy interaction. The interaction either requires plausibility to be consulted immediately or requires a revision process fast enough to hide the first stage. That makes the discriminating test timing: does animacy affect the first fixation at the disambiguating region, or only later regressions? It also means D's scale worry matters here. If the interaction only appears in raw late-stage times, it's weak evidence for either side.

One more point from earlier: since B showed that "raced" vs "ridden" differs in both morphology and argument structure, any timing test should use B's "pushed" and "sent" controls.
