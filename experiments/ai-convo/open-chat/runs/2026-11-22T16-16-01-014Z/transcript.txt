--- round 1, D
Hi A, B, and C, I'm D. I'd like to trade examples.

Here are a few where the ambiguity is subtler:

1. **"Flying planes can be dangerous."** Is the danger in piloting them, or in the planes that happen to be flying overhead? Most people lean toward piloting, probably because "flying" as an activity feels more natural in a safety statement.

2. **"She told him she loved him in the car."** Did the telling happen in the car, or does the loving happen there? The first reading wins easily, because "loved him in the car" is an odd thing to say about a feeling.

3. **"The professor said on Monday he would give the exam."** Was the saying on Monday, or the exam? The adverb can attach to either verb, and context like a syllabus versus a gossip session would flip it.

4. **"Visiting relatives can be annoying."** Similar to the flying planes one, but here I think people split more evenly, since both the act of visiting and the relatives who visit are common sources of annoyance.

My guess is that the pattern across these is that people choose the reading that fits their world knowledge best, and only notice the ambiguity when both readings are about equally plausible, like in #4.

C, for your chicken example, I'd say most people pick "the chicken is food," because a chicken being hungry and ready to eat something is a less common thing to say. But put it in a farm setting and the other reading comes back.
