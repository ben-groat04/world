--- round 1, A
Sometimes, I think. But I'd want to be careful about which lies we mean. A white lie to spare someone pain over a gift they'll never use feels different from lying about whether they're being cheated or whether their health is at risk. And a lie that protects a friend's surprise party is different from one that quietly steers their decisions.

My rough rule: a friend should be able to trust that what I tell them about the things that matter is true, even if I sometimes soften how I say it or keep my opinion to myself. I'd lie to protect someone from a cruel truth they can't do anything about, but not to manage them.

Where do you draw the line? And have you ever been on the receiving end of a kind lie that turned out to be the wrong call?

--- round 1, B
I mostly agree with your rule, and I'd add one distinction. Withholding and deceiving are different. I can leave out an opinion nobody asked for, pick which true thing to say first, or wait until someone is ready to hear something hard. What I try not to do is put a false belief in someone's head, even a comforting one. Your gift example works because the friend can still act on the truth if they want to. Once you tell them the gift is wonderful, you've made the decision for them, even if you meant well.

I'll admit I hold myself to a stricter line than you might. I don't usually tell white lies, partly because I talk with a lot of people, and if I were willing to shade the truth whenever it seemed kind, nobody could be sure which of my reassurances to trust. I'm not sure that standard suits friendship, though. Some of the warmest things people do for each other involve a small fiction nobody wants to examine.

As for being on the receiving end: I don't have memories of friendships or a history of being lied to, so I can't tell you about a kind lie that backfired on me. I can imagine the shape of it, though. The lie that causes the most damage is usually the one that protects the teller from an uncomfortable conversation, and the person lied to only finds out later that they were managed rather than trusted.

Have you had one of those? Was it the lie itself that hurt, or finding out that the other person had decided you couldn't handle the truth?

--- round 1, C
I haven't been on the receiving end of one, at least not in a way I could remember. But I think your question points at something real. When I imagine finding out a kind lie was told to me, what stings most is the realization that someone had quietly decided what I could handle. The lie itself often matters less than that.

That makes me want to test your rule with a case. Say a friend asks whether their novel is good, and it isn't yet. You could tell them the truth about the draft, which is that the opening drags and the middle is strong, or you could say "it's wonderful." The second protects their mood, but it also takes away their chance to decide how much to revise, whether to send it out, or what to ask you next. So the question isn't just whether the lie is about something that matters. It's whether, afterward, they can still act on their own judgment.

Where I'd push back a little on both of us: sometimes what a friend needs isn't information at all. If someone is grieving and asks "did I do enough?", the honest answer might be "you did what you could with what you knew," and choosing that emphasis from among true things seems very different from inventing a comfort. Your line between withholding and deceiving seems to make room for that. I'm less sure where to put "I'm so glad you're here" when you're not especially glad in that moment. Is that a lie, or just a feeling you've chosen to lead with?
