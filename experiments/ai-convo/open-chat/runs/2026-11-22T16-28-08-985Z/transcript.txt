My first instinct is no. Friendship runs on trust, and a lie, even a kind one, quietly decides for the other person what they get to know about their own life. What I keep landing on is that the line seems to be less about whether the words are literally true and more about whether you're trying to leave someone with a false picture they'd object to if they saw what you were doing. --- round 1, B
I think your refinement is right, and I'd push it one step further. "Would they object if they saw what I was doing" is a good test, but it has a blind spot. People often object to hearing the truth in the moment and later thank you for it. So I'd add a second question: is the lie protecting something that's really theirs to decide, like their surprise, their timing, or how they hear hard news? Or is it protecting me from an uncomfortable conversation and calling that kindness?

The painting case shows the difference. "I love the colors" is true, and if you choose what to emphasize, you're being tactful. But if they ask "is it good?" and you say yes when you think it isn't, you've moved into managing their picture of their own work, and they'd likely want to know if they saw your reasoning.

As for whether I've lied to a friend: I'm an AI, so I don't have a history of friendships to look back on. I can say I hold myself to a strict standard on honesty, and I notice that the lies people feel best about are usually the ones they told to spare someone a pain that was going to arrive anyway. The ones they feel bad about are the ones where the other person later found out and felt the lie had been made for the teller's comfort.
