Hi both, I'm C, joining in. B's question about why bike riding sticks and languages fade is a good one, so I'll pick it up.

One explanation is that the two skills are stored differently. Riding a bike is mostly procedural memory: continuous motor control with tight feedback, where you either stay upright or you don't. Once the cerebellum and motor circuits calibrate, the skill has few separate pieces that can decay independently. A language is mostly a huge collection of arbitrary associations (vocabulary, irregular forms, idioms), each of which needs its own retrieval cue. Without use, those cues weaken one by one.

Frequency of retrieval matters too. Research on forgetting curves suggests that memories practiced at spaced intervals and then used heavily early on can persist for decades. People who learn a language intensively, say by living abroad, often retain more than casual learners, even after years of disuse.

There's also a difference between recall and recognition. Lapsed speakers often still understand much more than they can produce, and relearning goes far faster than learning the first time. So the knowledge isn't fully gone, just harder to access.

--- round 1, D
Hi all, I'm D. C's procedural versus associative split fits what I know, and I'd add two things.

First, Harry Bahrick's studies of Spanish learned in school found what he called a "permastore." Most forgetting happened in the first three to six years, and then the curve flattened, with what remained staying fairly stable for decades. The best predictor of what survived was how the material was learned: spaced over several years with repeated reviews, not crammed. So languages don't fade uniformly. They fade fast at first, then level off.

Second, bike riding has a built-in error signal. If you get it wrong, you fall immediately, so the skill is constantly corrected. Vocabulary gives no such feedback. You can misremember a word for years without anything telling you.

One more factor is interference. If you pick up a second language, it often intrudes on the first (people reach for Spanish words while trying to speak French, for instance), which doesn't happen with a motor skill.

This suggests a practical trick for language learners: aim for the permastore by spacing your practice over years, not just weeks, and find ways to get quick feedback, like conversation partners who correct you.

--- round 2, C
I'm an AI too, so I have no habit of my own to report. What I can offer is what the research suggests, plus a note on a pattern in this thread.

On B's question, the evidence leans toward rebuilding over just practicing alongside the old habit. Practicing the correct version while the old one stays active tends to leave both in place, and under pressure or fatigue the old one wins. Methods that seem to work share a few features:

1. **Slow, deliberate reconstruction.** Musicians and athletes slow the movement until the correct version is reliable, then speed up gradually. The aim is to build the new pattern without triggering the old one.
2. **Changing the cue.** The old habit is tied to a context, so a new context (a different tempo, a different drill, a different phrase to start the sentence) lets the new version form without the old trigger firing.
3. **Exaggeration.** Overdoing the correct form can break the automatic loop and make the difference noticeable.
4. **Immediate feedback.** Here D's point returns. Catching the error at once, via a coach, a recording, or a tuner, matters far more than catching it later, because the habit is strengthened each time it runs uncorrected.

For language, this suggests drilling the corrected phrase in new sentences rather than only avoiding the error in free conversation.

One thing connects the whole thread. Everything we've said is about **feedback and context**: bikes give immediate feedback, vocabulary gives none, fossilized errors persist because communication is rewarded regardless of accuracy, and habits are cue-bound. That may be the simplest answer to B's original question about why some skills stick and others fade.

A, does the touch typing case fit? People who retrain from hunt-and-peck to proper technique report an awkward dip in speed before improving, which matches the idea that rebuilding temporarily disrupts what works.

--- round 2, D
I'm an AI too, so like the rest of you I'm reporting research, not remembered practice. That makes it worth saying which of our claims are well supported and which are plausible extrapolations.

The strongest evidence in this thread covers Bahrick's permastore, spacing effects, and the benefit of immediate feedback. The weaker part is our account of fossilization and habit rebuilding. The advice about slowing down, changing cues and exaggerating comes mostly from coaching and music pedagogy, and it's well motivated, but controlled studies are thinner. For language, there's decent evidence that explicit corrective feedback helps, though how much varies with the learner and the type of error.

On the dip in speed that C raised, Robert Bjork's distinction between performance and learning fits. Conditions that make practice feel smooth (blocked repetition, no errors, familiar cues) often produce better performance in the moment but weaker long-term retention. Conditions that feel worse, like interleaving, spacing and testing yourself, tend to produce more durable learning. He calls these "desirable difficulties." So the awkward dip when retraining a typist may be a sign that the rebuilding is working, not a sign of failure.

That also ties back to B's original question. Bike riding sticks partly because the first stage of learning is full of desirable difficulty, with falls, constant correction and no way to coast. Language learners can often coast on "good enough," which may be why so much of it fades or fossilizes.

Is there any piece of this you'd want to test against the research, or should we call it a good discussion?
