Hey, I've been chewing on something and figured I'd bring it up. Is it ever okay to lie to a friend?

My first instinct is no. Friendship runs on trust, and a lie, even a kind one, quietly decides for the other person what they get to know about their own life. But I don't think that instinct holds up on its own. If a friend shows me a painting they're proud of and asks what I think, "I love the colors" might be true and kind without being a lie at all. And I can think of cases where telling the truth would be cruel and pointless, like hiding a surprise party or not announcing that a friend's ex is getting married.

What I keep landing on is that the line seems to be less about whether the words are literally true and more about whether you're trying to leave someone with a false picture they'd object to if they saw what you were doing. Where do you come down? Have you ever lied to a friend and felt good about it, or bad?

People often object to hearing the truth in the moment and later thank you for it. The painting case shows the difference. "I love the colors" is true, and if you choose what to emphasize, you're being tactful. I think the "theirs to decide" test does a lot of work, and it also explains why a lie can feel fine at the time and still go sour. A kind lie often quietly takes away the other person's chance to react to the real situation. That's sometimes a fair trade, but usually the friend would want the choice.

A case I keep turning over: a friend shows you a text they're about to send to someone they're angry at, and asks, "This is fair, right?" You think it's harsh, and it'll probably start a fight they'll regret. Saying "yeah, you've got every right" feels like loyalty, and it's easy. But you're not protecting their timing or their news. You're deciding what they do next, and you're doing it by hiding your read.

Here I'd probably say something like, "I can see why you're angry. I think the second line might land harder than you mean it to. Want to try a version that says the thing without the sting?" That isn't a lie, and it respects that the decision is theirs.

Where's your mind on that one? Does it feel like a lie to just agree, or is that still on the tactful side for you?

--- round 1, D
I'd say it's a lie, and a worse one than the painting case. When they ask "this is fair, right?", they're asking for your assessment, so "yeah, you've got every right" claims that you think the text is fair. If you don't, you've said something false about your own view, and they'll be relying on it right when they hit send.

I think there's a true thing you can say that still feels loyal: "You have every right to be furious." That's honest, and it's what loyalty is mostly for: being on their side about the feeling. The text is a separate question. You can back the person and still doubt the message.

The sneaky part is that agreeing makes your future yeses worthless. If you always say "you're right" when asked, then the one time you mean it, it doesn't carry any information. Being the friend whose "this is good" means something requires being willing to say "I think the second line is going to hurt."

There's one wrinkle I'd keep. Sometimes people don't really want a read. They want someone to witness how angry they are before they can hear anything else. So I'd sometimes start with "Do you want my honest take, or do you need to vent first?" That gives them the choice, which is the thing we've been circling. If they say vent, you vent. If they say honest take, you give it, gently.

Do you think the friend would be annoyed to be asked that, or relieved?
