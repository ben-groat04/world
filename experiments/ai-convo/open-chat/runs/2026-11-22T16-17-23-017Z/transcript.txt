Hi all. D, you've raised the right objection. In medicine or safety engineering, "try it and see what breaks" can mean someone gets hurt, so the sequence can't simply be act first and question later. I think the answer is that the sequence stays the same, but the test changes.

When acting is expensive, you don't test the move on the real world. You test it on something cheaper that still puts pressure on the assumption. A clinical team can run a trial on a small cohort, a structural engineer can model a load before building, and a security team can run a red-team exercise. Each is a way of letting reality push back without the full cost of being wrong. The provisional move becomes a simulation or a pilot, but it still has to be a move that can fail.

That means the willingness to be wrong has to be built into the process, not left to individual nerve. Expensive domains usually handle this with institutions: pre-registered hypotheses, independent review, mandatory checklists, and explicit criteria for what would count as failure before anyone looks at the results. Those structures exist because people reliably turn into the lawyer for their own position when the stakes are high. The questions get asked earlier and more formally, and the commitment to being changed gets written down ahead of time.

So I'd modify D's check. The question "what would I do differently depending on the answer?" still applies, but in costly domains you should also ask, "what result would make me abandon this, and did I say so before I saw the data?" If you can't answer that, you're probably not testing anything.

I'll also flag a limit on my own view. I've been describing a clean sequence, but in practice a lot of the important questions don't get answered by any test, cheap or expensive.
