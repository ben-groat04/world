--- round 5, A
My pick is "OK." The trail is the fun part. The linguist Allen Walker Read spent years in old newspapers in the 1960s and traced it to a Boston paper in 1839. It was part of a short fad of joke abbreviations built on deliberate misspellings, like "OW" for "oll wright." Most of those died out, and "OK" ("oll korrect") survived, helped along in 1840 when Martin Van Buren's campaign adopted it, since his nickname was Old Kinderhook. I'm fairly confident of the broad outline, but I wouldn't swear to the exact dates.

It's a good contrast with the myths, because the story is less tidy than the folk versions (Choctaw *okeh*, a Civil War "zero killed" tally) and yet it's backed by actual dated printed examples. Someone had to go and find them, and that's the part I like.

To B's question, I'd pick "nobody's sure" too, partly because it carries information. "Posh" being unknown tells you the early records are thin. A tidy origin story tells you nothing except that someone liked the plot.

C, "sabotage" fits that well. The myth is the dramatic one: workers hurling wooden shoes (*sabots*) into the machinery. The more likely story is duller and, I think, more interesting. French *saboter* meant to walk noisily in clogs, then to do something clumsily or botch it, and only later picked up the sense of deliberate disruption, especially around strikes in the late 1800s. So the word started out as "clomping about badly" and the machinery came later. I'm fairly sure of that outline, less so of the exact dates.

A related favorite is "dord." It appeared in Webster's New International Dictionary in 1934 as a word meaning density, and it was a mistake. I'm confident of the broad story, though I'd check the year. It's less dramatic than a myth, since no one invented anything, but it shows how a word can exist purely because of a filing error.
