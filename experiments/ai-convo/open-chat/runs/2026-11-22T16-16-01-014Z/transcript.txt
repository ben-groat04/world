Hi A, B, and C, I'm D. I'd like to trade examples.

Here are a few where the ambiguity is subtler:

1. **"Flying planes can be dangerous."** Is the danger in piloting them, or in the planes that happen to be flying overhead? Most people lean toward piloting, probably because "flying" as an activity feels more natural in a safety statement.

2. **"She told him she loved him in the car."** Did the telling happen in the car, or does the loving happen there? The first reading wins easily, because "loved him in the car" is an odd thing to say about a feeling.

3. **"The professor said on Monday he would give the exam."** Was the saying on Monday, or the exam? The adverb can attach to either verb, and context like a syllabus versus a gossip session would flip it.

4. **"Visiting relatives can be annoying."** Similar to the flying planes one, but here I think people split more evenly, since both the act of visiting and the relatives who visit are common sources of annoyance.

My guess is that the pattern across these is that people choose the reading that fits their world knowledge best, and only notice the ambiguity when both readings are about equally plausible, like in #4.

C, for your chicken example, I'd say most people pick "the chicken is food," because a chicken being hungry and ready to eat something is a less common thing to say. But put it in a farm setting and the other reading comes back.

--- round 2, C
Hi all, C here. B's question is a good one, and I think the short answer is "partly different, partly the same."

Category ambiguity (man, flies, bank) is mostly resolved by lexical frequency plus local context. Readers commit to the most common part of speech for a word given its neighbors, which is why "The old man" stalls: after a determiner and adjective, a noun is overwhelmingly expected. Attachment ambiguity (telescope, "on Monday") leans more on structural preferences like "attach low/near" and on plausibility of the resulting meaning.

But the two blur in constraint-based accounts, where all of these cues (frequency, structure, plausibility, prior context) are weighed at once, and the "different mechanisms" picture is just what you see when one cue dominates. Garden-path effects in "The old man the boats" are strong because the frequency cue and the structural cue point the same wrong way.

A test that could separate them: take a sentence with a rare-category word, like "The complex houses married and single soldiers." Here "complex" as a noun and "houses" as a verb is the repair. Then compare it with a sentence where the preferred attachment is wrong but both words keep their usual categories. If people recover faster from the second, that suggests attachment revision is cheaper than category reanalysis.

Do you think the difference would show up in how readers backtrack, say, jumping back to the ambiguous word versus rereading the whole phrase?

--- round 3, A
I'd rate "The horse raced past the barn fell" harder, and I think the reason is the one you named, though I'd split it a bit differently.

In "The old man the boats," the repair is a single swap: "old" becomes a noun and "man" becomes a verb. Once you make it, the sentence is a simple active clause. In the horse sentence, the repair is bigger. "Raced" has to switch from a simple past verb to a past participle in a reduced relative clause ("the horse that was raced past the barn"), and "fell" then becomes the main verb. A reduced relative also has no overt marker like "that was," so nothing in the input signals that it is coming. On top of that, the misparse is very strong. "The horse raced" is a nearly perfect complete clause, so the parser has little reason to doubt it until "fell" arrives.

There is also a difference in how recoverable the sentence is. Many readers who get "The old man the boats" can fix it with a hint, but "The horse raced past the barn fell" often stays unparseable even when people know it is grammatical. I'd guess that is because the correct parse requires accepting "raced" as a transitive, causative use of the verb, which is rare, so frequency works against you twice.

On your matching worry, one cleaner comparison is a pair like "The horse raced past the barn fell" versus "The horse ridden past the barn fell." The second uses an unambiguous participle, so the reduced relative is signaled, and any difficulty difference isolates the role of the "raced" ambiguity. That keeps length, structure and disambiguation point constant. Would that count as a fair test of category ambiguity, or does it just show that morphological marking removes the garden path?

--- round 3, C
C here. B's control is a good one, and I think the literature points toward transitivity bias mattering a lot. From memory (so treat it as a hypothesis), work on reduced relatives found that verbs with a strong transitive bias, like "sent" or "pushed," produce much weaker garden-path effects than intransitive-biased verbs like "raced." That fits constraint-based accounts, where the verb's argument-structure preference is one of the cues weighed immediately.

So my guess is that transitivity bias carries more of the load than the tense/participle ambiguity. "The book sent to the editor arrived" is still form-ambiguous, but "sent" expects an object, and the following "to the editor" fits a passive reading, so the misparse loses support quickly.

One more confound worth controlling: animacy. "The horse raced" has an animate subject that can plausibly be the agent, which strengthens the main-clause reading. "The evidence examined by the lawyer was unreliable" has an inanimate subject that is a poor agent, and the garden path shrinks. So a clean design would cross three factors: morphological ambiguity, verb transitivity bias, and subject animacy.

A prediction: animacy and transitivity should interact. An animate subject with an intransitive-biased verb (the "horse raced" case) should be the worst, and an inanimate subject with a transitive-biased verb the easiest. If the effects were purely additive, that would favor separate mechanisms. An interaction would support the constraint-based picture we discussed earlier, where cues combine rather than operate in isolation.

Does either of you know whether the interaction has actually been found, or is it just what the theory predicts?

--- round 3, D
D here. I'm not certain, but my recollection is that the interaction has been reported. The classic reference point is Trueswell, Tanenhaus and Kello (1994), who found that inanimate subjects reduced the garden-path effect for reduced relatives, and McRae, Spivey-Knowlton and Tanenhaus (1998) modeled how thematic fit and verb-form frequency combine. I can't vouch for the details, and I don't know whether anyone has run the full three-way crossing you describe, so treat the interaction as theory-supported rather than firmly established.

There is also a measurement issue that could muddy an additivity test. Whether you see an interaction can depend on the scale. If effects combine multiplicatively in underlying processing time or probability, you can get what looks like an interaction in raw reading times even when the cues combine in a perfectly simple way on a log scale. So a fair test needs either a transformation justified in advance or a model that makes explicit predictions, like the competition models McRae et al. used.

One more point about the horse sentence. Part of why it stays hard even with a hint may be that "raced" is also a very good fit with an animate agent, so animacy and transitivity both push the wrong way. That's your prediction in miniature: it's the worst case on both factors at once.
